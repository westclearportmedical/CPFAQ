The problem

A thin, flexible tube placed in an upper-arm vein and threaded toward the heart.

A PICC (peripherally inserted central catheter, said "pick") stays in for weeks or months to deliver chemotherapy, IV antibiotics, parenteral nutrition and other infusions, often at home.

The part outside the body is what goes wrong in daily life: the juncture hub, the extension legs (one per lumen), the clamps and the end connectors. The CP-100 deals only with that external part.

Often enough to be a routine problem, even with today's securement in place.

2.3–8%of PICCs dislodge in recent studies, more often in lines with more lumens1
8.9%accidental withdrawal in one prospective cohort of 192 PICCs2
7.9%dislodgement with StatLock in a pooled NICE analysis (63 of 793)3
61%of patients report at least one line problem after discharge; 26% restrict daily activities4

These rates are measured with standard securement and a transparent dressing already on. They are the failures that remain, and the ones the CP-100 is designed to reduce.

A line that migrates or comes out usually means some combination of:

  • an extra nurse visit, or a trip to a clinic or emergency department
  • a replacement line, with another insertion procedure
  • delayed or interrupted chemotherapy, antibiotics or nutrition
  • in some cases, readmission to hospital

Lifted, soaked or snagged dressings cause a second, more frequent problem: unscheduled dressing changes between the routine weekly visits.

  • INS 2024, Standard 39: use a securement method in addition to the primary dressing. Inadequate securement can cause dislodgement and premature removal (Level I evidence).5
  • INS 2024, Standard 53: use a securement product or a taped tension loop of tubing to reduce the risk of accidental dislodgement.5
  • CDC: replace transparent dressings on central lines at least every 7 days, or sooner if damp, loosened or soiled.6

The CP-100 is built around these three practices: an extra securement layer, a managed tubing loop, and a device that is changed with the weekly dressing.

Existing devices anchor the catheter. Nothing protects the rest of the external line.

Adhesive anchors such as StatLock hold the catheter near the insertion site. The extension legs, clamps and connectors are left loose. At home they catch on clothing, bedding and door handles, pull on the insertion site, and get wet in the shower. Patients often improvise with tape or buy separate sleeves and shower covers.

The CP-100 is designed for that gap: the whole external line, in the setting where most of the dwell time happens.

The CP-100

A single-use, non-sterile, skin-worn device that secures and shrouds the external part of an indwelling PICC or central line.

It holds the juncture hub, extension legs, clamps and end connectors in one low-profile housing on the upper arm (for a PICC) or the neck or chest (for a central line). The current design is the CP-100 V4.

Proposed indication: "The CP-100 is intended to secure and protect the external portion of an indwelling intravascular catheter (PICC or central venous catheter) to the patient's skin."

Each feature maps to a specific failure seen in practice.

  • Anchors the hubSeats the catheter hub so tugs on the tubing are not transmitted to the insertion site, adding the extra securement INS recommends.
  • Manages the tubingCoils excess tubing into a defined channel, acting as the tension loop INS describes and keeping sharp bends and kinks out of the line.
  • Shrouds the portsSilicone caps protect the end connectors from knocks, soiling and splash, and stay accessible for flushes and infusions.
  • Keeps the site visibleA clear window over the insertion site and dressing allows visual checks at any time without removing anything.

  • Adhesive carrier that bonds to clean, dry, intact skin around the site. The adhesive comes from a specialist in adhesive medical wearables.
  • Semi-rigid moulded clear chassis with a hub cradle, a concave tubing channel with a retention lip, and a clear window of about 40 mm over the insertion site.
  • Liquid silicone (LSR) port caps that shroud the end connectors.
  • Low profile: about 12 mm high, under a 15 mm design limit, so it sits under everyday clothing.
  • Modular: one common chassis with interchangeable 2-port and 3-port blocks, so a single platform covers different line types.

The device is latex-free and labeled non-sterile.

The nurse places the line, the dressing and the securement device exactly as today, and applies the CP-100 over them. Facility line-care protocols stay the same. It can also be used alongside subcutaneous anchors such as SecurAcath.

No.

The CP-100 does not connect to the catheter, the connectors' fluid path or the insertion site. Because there is no fluid-path connection, small-bore connector standards (ISO 80369-7) do not apply. Flushes, infusions and blood draws go through the ports as usual, with the caps opened in place.

The CP-100 is non-sterile and worn over intact skin and over the existing dressing. Clearport makes no infection-prevention claim. Its function is mechanical: securement, tubing and port protection, site visibility and splash resistance.

Use cases

Ambulatory adults with a PICC or central line managed at home or as outpatients.

  • Oncology: lines for chemotherapy and frequent blood draws, often kept for 4–6 months. The longest-wearing group.
  • OPAT: outpatient IV antibiotic courses through home infusion, typically a few weeks.
  • Home parenteral nutrition, hydration, inotropes and other long-term home infusions.

Patients at higher risk, such as very active, confused or long-dwell patients, carry the most benefit.

  • Hospital discharge: applied before the patient goes home, as part of a discharge kit, when risk is highest.
  • Home infusion and home health: changed by the visiting nurse at the weekly dressing change.
  • Infusion centers and oncology day care: supplied in bulk for patients between visits.
  • Hospital-at-home programs that manage the line through the whole course.

By the vascular-access, infusion, oncology or home-health nurse, in four steps. Nothing is threaded over the line.

  1. Peel and placeRemove the backing and press the adhesive base onto intact skin, with the window framing the insertion site and dressing.
  2. Seat the hubPress the catheter hub into the cradle so it sits flat and steady, taking the tug off the insertion site.
  3. Coil the tubingRoute the extension legs into the channel, flat and off the skin.
  4. Cap and checkClose the silicone caps over the ports and check the site through the window.

The design target is under two minutes per application. Application time is measured in the clinical study.

  • Routine: replaced with the dressing on its regular schedule, typically about once a week for transparent dressings.
  • Sooner if it lifts at the edges, gets wet underneath, or is visibly soiled or damaged.
  • Between changes it stays on through flushes, infusions, showering and sleep.
  • Single-use: a new device at every change, never reapplied.

A typical home course uses about six devices. The first month after discharge is planned as a 4-device kit.

Proposed wear schedule. Final instructions for use will be confirmed in the patient and nurse validation study.

The line is held flat under one cover instead of hanging loose. Patients can dress, sleep and move without the tubing catching, and the design resists splashes and water ingress for showering. The site stays visible, so a caregiver can check it without lifting anything.

Comfort, showering, sleep and daily activities are all measured in the clinical study.

  • Apply only to clean, dry, intact skin. Do not place over broken or irritated skin.
  • Check the skin under the adhesive at each change for signs of adhesive-related irritation (MARSI).
  • Non-sterile and single-use.
  • Remove the device and assess the line if dislodgement is suspected.
  • Always used with, never instead of, the primary dressing and facility line-care protocols.

Intended use and precautions are proposed and will be finalized with the instructions for use.

Evidence & regulatory

Expected Class I, 510(k)-exempt, as an intravascular catheter securement device.

The expected classification is 21 CFR 880.5210, product code KMK. On that basis the CP-100 is registered and listed with the FDA rather than submitted for premarket clearance. Its quality file still includes design controls, risk management (ISO 14971), biocompatibility testing for intact skin (ISO 10993), bench verification and UDI.

The bench program covers peel and hub-pull strength, 7-day wear, water ingress, cap retention and fit on the catheters listed above.

Preliminary. To be confirmed by regulatory counsel. Not a regulatory determination.

Market access

  • Users: vascular-access, infusion, oncology and home-health nurses, and patients and caregivers at home.
  • Buyers: home infusion providers, hospital oncology and OPAT programs (through value analysis committees), integrated delivery networks and payers.
  • Patients directly, through Clearport's own storefront and Amazon, with FSA/HSA eligibility planned.

Most of the cost of a failed line falls on the payer, not the provider who changes the dressing. Clearport's value case is therefore built around payers, Medicare Advantage plans and integrated systems, with home infusion providers as the channel to patients.

No billing code pays for the device itself.

Its cost falls within existing supply payments:

  • Commercial home infusion: S5501, the per diem for complex catheter care (more than one lumen), which includes supplies. Not payable by Medicare.
  • Medicare Part B (DME): A4221, weekly supplies for a non-insulin drug infusion catheter, only with a covered external infusion pump.
  • Hospital: packaged into the inpatient DRG or outpatient APC payment.

Clearport is pursuing payer contracts and a billing route as a non-routine supply.
